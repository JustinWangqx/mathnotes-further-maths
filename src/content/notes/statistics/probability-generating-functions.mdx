## A distribution as a function

For a nonnegative integer-valued random variable $X$, its probability generating function is

$$G_X(t)=E(t^X)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P(X=k)t^k.$$

The coefficient of $t^k$ is $P(X=k)$. Check that the coefficients are nonnegative and $G_X(1)=1$. A finite-support distribution produces a polynomial.

## Recover moments

When the corresponding moments exist,

$$E(X)=G_X'(1),\qquad E[X(X-1)]=G_X''(1),$$
$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=G_X''(1)+G_X'(1)-[G_X'(1)]^2.$$

The second derivative gives a factorial moment, not $E(X^2)$ directly. The extra first derivative supplies the missing $E(X)$.

## Example 1: read and differentiate

Let $G_X(t)=1/4+3t/4$. This represents $P(X=0)=1/4$ and $P(X=1)=3/4$.

Since $G_X'(1)=3/4$ and $G_X''(1)=0$,

$$E(X)=\frac34,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac34-\left(\frac34\right)^2=\frac3{16}.$$

This agrees with a Bernoulli distribution of success probability $3/4$.

## Independent sums

If $X$ and $Y$ are independent and $Z=X+Y$,

$$G_Z(t)=G_X(t)G_Y(t).$$

Independence allows the expectation of $t^Xt^Y$ to factor. Without it, the product rule need not hold. For a sum of $n$ independent identically distributed variables, the PGF is $[G_X(t)]^n$.

## Example 2: combine two trials

Let $X\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2)$ and $Y\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/4)$ independently. Then

$$G_{X+Y}(t)=\left(\frac12+\frac12t\right)\left(\frac34+\frac14t\right)=\frac38+\frac12t+\frac18t^2.$$

Thus $P(X+Y=2)=1/8$. The mean is $3/4$ and the variance is $1/4+3/16=7/16$, also obtained by differentiating the product.

## Familiar forms

For $X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$, $G_X(t)=(1-p+pt)^n$. For a geometric variable counting trials until the first success, supported on $1,2,\ldots$,

$$G_X(t)=\frac{pt}{1-(1-p)t},\qquad |(1-p)t|<1.$$

A geometric variable counting failures instead starts at zero and has a different numerator. State the convention before using a memorised PGF.

## Common mistakes

Check support, normalisation and independence before manipulating the function. Do not confuse the coefficient of a power with the value of the function at that power, or identify $G''(1)$ as the variance.
