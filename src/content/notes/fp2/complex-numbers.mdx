## The idea

A complex number is both an algebraic object and a point in a plane. Cartesian form is useful for adding; polar form makes multiplication, powers and roots much easier to see.

For $z=x+iy$, the **modulus** is $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. An **argument** $\theta$ is an angle measured anticlockwise from the positive real axis, so

$$z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=re^{i\theta}.$$

For $z\ne0$, arguments differ by integer multiples of $2\pi$. We use the principal argument convention $-\pi<\operatorname{Arg}(z)\le\pi$. Zero has no defined argument.

## Arithmetic and geometry

If $z_1=r_1e^{i\theta_1}$ and $z_2=r_2e^{i\theta_2}$, then

$$z_1z_2=r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)},\qquad \frac{z_1}{z_2}=\frac{r_1}{r_2}e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)}\quad(z_2\ne0).$$

Multiplication scales moduli and adds angles. Division divides moduli and subtracts angles.

The conjugate of $x+iy$ is $x-iy$, and $z\overline z=|z|^2$. This is why conjugates remove imaginary terms from a denominator:

$$\frac1z=\frac{\overline z}{|z|^2}\quad(z\ne0).$$

## De Moivre’s theorem

For an integer $n$,

$$[r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)]^n=r^n[\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta)].$$

For negative $n$, require $r>0$. When using fractional powers, list the possible arguments explicitly rather than assuming there is a single answer.

## Example 1: a complex power

Find $(1+i\sqrt3)^3$.

**Step 1. Find the modulus and argument.** The modulus is $2$. The point is in quadrant I with argument $\pi/3$.

$$1+i\sqrt3=2(\cos(\pi/3)+i\sin(\pi/3)).$$

**Step 2. Apply de Moivre.**

$$ (1+i\sqrt3)^3=8(\cos\pi+i\sin\pi)=\boxed{-8}.$$

The angle triples and the modulus is cubed. Those are separate operations.

## Finding every root

To solve $z^n=Re^{i\Theta}$ for $R>0$ and positive integer $n$, use

$$z_k=R^{1/n}\exp\!\left(i\frac{\Theta+2k\pi}{n}\right),\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1.$$

The $n$ roots lie evenly around a circle of radius $R^{1/n}$. The angular spacing is $2\pi/n$. Taking $k=n$ would repeat the first root.

## Example 2: cube roots of a negative number

Solve $z^3=-8$.

Write $-8=8e^{i(\pi+2k\pi)}$. The modulus of each root is $2$, and the three arguments are

$$\frac\pi3,\quad \pi,\quad \frac{5\pi}3.$$

Therefore,

$$\boxed{z=1+i\sqrt3,\quad -2,\quad 1-i\sqrt3.}$$

Each root has modulus $2$, and consecutive roots differ by $2\pi/3$. Cubing any one of them gives $-8$.

## Roots of unity and identities

The roots of $z^n=1$ are $e^{2k\pi i/n}$ for $k=0,\ldots,n-1$. They form a regular polygon on the unit circle. Translating an equation such as $(z-a)^n=R$ translates the resulting root diagram by $a$.

De Moivre also connects algebra to trigonometric identities. Expanding $(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^3$ and equating real parts gives

$$\cos3\theta=\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta=4\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta.$$

The imaginary parts similarly give $\sin3\theta=3\sin\theta-4\sin^3\theta$.

## Common mistakes

- Using a calculator’s inverse tangent without checking the quadrant.
- Mixing degrees and radians in an exponential expression.
- Multiplying the modulus by $n$ instead of raising it to the power $n$.
- Dividing only the principal argument when finding roots and losing the other solutions.
- Giving arguments outside the requested interval or counting the same root twice.

Sketch the roots on an Argand diagram as a quick check. The symmetry will often reveal a missing root or an incorrect angle.
