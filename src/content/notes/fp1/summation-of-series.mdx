## Two useful approaches

Polynomial terms often reduce to standard summations. Rational terms often simplify by partial fractions, revealing consecutive terms that cancel. Write a finite sum first; the infinite case comes from a limit of that finite result.

## Standard sums

For a positive integer $n$,

$$\sum_{r=1}^n r=\frac{n(n+1)}2,\quad \sum_{r=1}^n r^2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6,$$
$$\sum_{r=1}^n r^3=\left[\frac{n(n+1)}2\right]^2.$$

Expand a polynomial summand, then apply linearity. If a sum starts later, subtract the omitted initial terms.

## Example 1: expand a polynomial term

Find $\sum_{r=1}^n r(r+1)$.

$$\sum_{r=1}^n(r^2+r)=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6+\frac{n(n+1)}2.$$

Taking out $n(n+1)/6$ leaves $2n+4$, so

$$\boxed{\sum_{r=1}^n r(r+1)=\frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}3.}$$

Check $n=1$: both sides give $2$.

## The method of differences

If $u_r=v_r-v_{r+1}$, then

$$\sum_{r=1}^n u_r=v_1-v_{n+1}.$$

Write the first few terms and the last few terms to see the cancellation. For a gap larger than one, more boundary terms survive.

## Example 2: a telescoping sum

Use partial fractions:

$$\frac1{r(r+1)}=\frac1r-\frac1{r+1}.$$

Therefore,

$$S_n=(1-\tfrac12)+(\tfrac12-\tfrac13)+\cdots+(\tfrac1n-\tfrac1{n+1})=1-\frac1{n+1}.$$

Since $1/(n+1)\to0$, the infinite series converges to $1$.

## Convergence and care with indices

A series converges when its partial sums have a finite limit. Showing only that $u_r\to0$ is not enough: the harmonic series has terms tending to zero but diverges.

If parameters occur, inspect the final boundary term and state the parameter values for which its limit exists. Never discard a term simply because its index contains $n$.
