## Choose a substitution from the expression

The notes match radicals to identities. For $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$ use $x=a\sin\theta$ on a branch with $\cos\theta\ge0$. For $\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$ use $x=a\sinh t$, since $1+\sinh^2t=\cosh^2t$. For $\sqrt{x^2-a^2}$ on $x>a>0$, $x=a\cosh t$ with $t>0$ gives $a\sinh t$. Carry the differential and any integration limits through the substitution.

For $a>0$ the second choice makes $\int dx/\sqrt{a^2+x^2}=\int dt$, giving $\operatorname{arsinh}(x/a)+C$. For a quadratic without a radical, completing the square often exposes an inverse-function derivative. For example $x^2+2x+2=(x+1)^2+1$, so its reciprocal integrates to $\arctan(x+1)+C$.

## Parts and reduction formulas

Integrate the product rule $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$ and rearrange to get $\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du$. Choose $u$ so its derivative is simpler, as with a polynomial times an exponential.

For $I_n=\int_0^1x^ne^x\,dx$ with $n\ge1$, take $u=x^n$, $dv=e^x\,dx$. The boundary term is $e$, so $I_n=e-nI_{n-1}$. Start from $I_0=e-1$, then $I_1=1$ and $I_2=e-2$. For powers of trig or hyperbolic functions, use an identity after applying parts so the remaining integral has the same pattern at a lower power.

## Arc length and surface area

A short curve segment has length approximately $\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}$. In the limit the Cartesian length element is $\sqrt{1+(dy/dx)^2}\,dx$ for increasing $x$. With a parameter use $\sqrt{(dx/dt)^2+(dy/dt)^2}\,dt$. In polar coordinates the radial and angular contributions are perpendicular, giving $\sqrt{(dr/d\theta)^2+r^2}\,d\theta$.

For $y=\cosh x$ the square root simplifies to $\cosh x$, so the length from $0$ to $\ln2$ is $\sinh(\ln2)=3/4$.

When rotating the segment about an axis, it sweeps a thin band. Multiply its length by the circumference $2\pi R$, where $R$ is the distance to the axis. Integrate $2\pi R\,ds$. Rotation about the $x$-axis uses $R=|y|$, while rotation about the $y$-axis uses $R=|x|$.

## Compare a sum with areas

For a positive decreasing $f$, the rectangles with left endpoints sit above the curve. Those with right endpoints sit below it. This gives

$$
\int_1^{n+1} f(x)\,dx
\;\le\; \sum_{r=1}^n f(r)
\;\le\; f(1)+\int_1^n f(x)\,dx.
$$

With $f(x)=1/x^2$ the upper bound is at most $2$. Positive terms make the partial sums increasing, so the bounded sequence converges. With $f(x)=1/x$, the lower bound $\ln(n+1)$ grows without limit, which proves divergence.
