## Start with the sampling variation

A sample mean changes from sample to sample. For independent observations its variance is $\sigma^2/n$, so its standard deviation, called the standard error, is $\sigma/\sqrt n$. Subtract the mean specified by the null hypothesis and divide by the standard error. This expresses the observed difference in units of sampling variation.

For a small sample from a normal population with unknown variance, estimate $\sigma^2$ with $s^2=\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2/(n-1)$. The deviations from $\bar x$ sum to zero, so there are $n-1$ independent pieces of variation. Under $H_0$, the resulting statistic has a $t$ distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom. Its heavier tails account for the uncertainty in the estimated standard error.

$$
t=\frac{\bar x-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt n}.
$$

## Let the alternative choose the tail

Set the hypotheses before comparing with a critical value. An alternative $\mu<\mu_0$ uses the lower tail. An alternative $\mu>\mu_0$ uses the upper tail. A two-sided alternative divides the significance level between both tails. A $p$-value is a probability calculated under the null model, using outcomes at least as extreme in the direction or directions specified.

For six normally distributed wingspans, $8.8,\ 9.6,\ 9.2,\ 9.1,\ 9.9,\ 8.7$ cm, test whether the mean is below $9.4$ cm at 10%. The mean is $9.2167$ and the sample variance is $0.213667$, giving

$$
t=\frac{9.2167-9.4}{\sqrt{0.213667/6}}\approx-0.972.
$$

The lower-tail critical value for five degrees of freedom is $-1.476$. The observed statistic falls outside the rejection region. At the 10% level there is insufficient evidence that the mean wingspan is below $9.4$ cm.

## Compare two independent means

For independent samples, the variances of their means add when you take the difference. For large samples, use the estimated standard error $\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}$ and a normal approximation. With small independent samples from normal populations that share a variance, estimate that variance by pooling the two within-sample sums of squares.

$$
\begin{aligned}
s_p^2&=\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2},\\
\operatorname{SE}(\bar X_1-\bar X_2)&=s_p\sqrt{\frac1{n_1}+\frac1{n_2}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Each sample uses one degree of freedom to estimate its own mean. There are $n_1+n_2-2$ left for the pooled estimate and for the $t$ reference distribution. This pooling relies on the common-variance assumption.

## Reduce paired measurements to one sample

If measurements share a subject or are naturally matched, compute a difference for each pair in a fixed order. Then test the mean difference against zero using a one-sample $t$ statistic. The model assumption concerns the distribution of the differences and independence between pairs. Reversing the order reverses the sign of every difference, so reverse the direction of the alternative as well.

## Build and interpret a confidence interval

An interval for a mean or mean difference has the form $\text{estimate}\pm(\text{critical value})\times\operatorname{SE}$. Use the reference distribution and degrees of freedom from the relevant sampling model. A 95% procedure would produce intervals covering the fixed population parameter in 95% of repeated samples under the model assumptions.

To change the confidence level from a given interval, use its midpoint as the estimate. Its half-width divided by the original critical value recovers the standard error. Multiply that standard error by the new critical value. If the data were recoded as $Y=aX+b$, then $\bar y=a\bar x+b$ and $s_Y=|a|s_X$. Convert both interval endpoints back to the original scale and put them in increasing order.
