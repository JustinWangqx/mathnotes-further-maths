## Choose what information to keep

The sign test counts the directions of deviations from a hypothesised median. It can be used with independent observations from a continuous population without assuming symmetry. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test uses directions and the order of the magnitudes. For its one-sample form, assume the continuous population is symmetric about its centre. With paired data, work on the within-pair differences and apply the relevant assumptions to those differences.

## Why signs follow a binomial model

For a continuous distribution with median $m_0$, half of the probability lies on each side. Under the null, independent observations therefore give independent signs with positive probability $1/2$. Subtract $m_0$, omit zero differences as directed, and use the number of remaining signs as $n$. The positive count $S$ has the null distribution $B(n,1/2)$.

If 12 of 15 observations lie above 135 and three below, the two tails are equally likely under the null. The two-sided $p$-value is

$$
2P(S\le3)=2\frac{1+15+105+455}{2^{15}}\approx0.0352.
$$

This gives evidence at the 5% level that the median differs from 135. For a one-sided alternative that the median is higher, use the probability of at least as many positive signs.

## Why signed ranks add information

Rank the absolute deviations from smallest to largest and restore the original signs. When magnitudes tie, assign their average rank and follow the stated procedure for ties. Let $P$ and $Q$ be the sums of ranks for positive and negative deviations, with both sums recorded as non-negative numbers. Their total is $n(n+1)/2$. Under a symmetric null, conditional on the magnitudes, each sign is equally likely in either direction.

For the mackerel example, testing against $1.8$ kg gives negative ranks $2$ and $7$, hence $Q=9$ and $P=55-9=46$. A shift upwards makes the negative rank sum small. At the one-tailed 5% critical value of 10 for ten observations, this provides evidence that the median weight exceeds $1.8$ kg. For a two-sided test, either unusually small sum counts as evidence.

## Approximate a large rank sum

For untied ranks, each rank $r$ contributes either $r$ or zero to $P$, with equal probability under the null. Add the means $r/2$ and the variances $r^2/4$ to get

$$
E(P)=\frac{n(n+1)}4,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(P)=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{24}.
$$

When the normal approximation is appropriate, standardise using these moments. Use a continuity correction for integer rank sums. For instance, $P(P\le q)$ is approximated using a boundary of $q+0.5$. Choose the tail from the alternative and from which rank sum you are using.

## Work with paired data

For before-and-after measurements, choose a fixed subtraction such as “after minus before”. A positive difference then means an increase. The paired sign test counts increases, while the matched-pairs signed-rank test ranks the absolute changes and gives larger changes more weight. Rank the differences across the pairs, keeping the original pairing intact.
