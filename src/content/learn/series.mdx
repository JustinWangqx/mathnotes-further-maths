## From terms to partial sums

The index $r$ tells you which term to substitute. The upper limit $n$ tells you where to stop. For example, $1+3+\cdots+(2n-1)=\sum_{r=1}^n(2r-1)$. Split the sum into $2\sum r-\sum1$ to get $n(n+1)-n=n^2$.

## Why the standard power sums work

The notes obtain the power sums by expanding successive differences. Write out $(r+1)^3-r^3=3r^2+3r+1$ and sum from $1$ to $n$. On the left, every middle cube cancels. The result is

$$
(n+1)^3-1
=3\sum_{r=1}^n r^2+3\frac{n(n+1)}2+n.
$$

Rearranging gives $\sum r^2=n(n+1)(2n+1)/6$. Using $(r+1)^4-r^4$ and the already-known sums similarly gives $\sum r^3=[n(n+1)/2]^2$. If a sum starts at $r=m$, subtract the partial sum up to $m-1$.

## The method of differences

Rewrite each term as a difference of nearby expressions. For example, partial fractions give

$$
\frac1{r(r+1)}=\frac1r-\frac1{r+1}.
$$

Writing out the first two terms and the last term shows exactly what remains:

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_n&=(1-\tfrac12)+(\tfrac12-\tfrac13)+\cdots+
(\tfrac1n-\tfrac1{n+1})\\
&=1-\frac1{n+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

With a difference such as $f(r)-f(r+2)$, two terms survive at each end. The same pattern works with trigonometric differences after a product-to-sum identity has produced the matching expressions.

## Decide what happens at infinity

The infinite sum is the limit of the *finite partial sums*. In the example above, $1/(n+1)\to0$ and the sum is $1$. A geometric partial sum tends to a finite value when the nonzero first term has common ratio with $|q|<1$. A telescoping boundary term that oscillates or grows can stop the sum from converging. For instance $\sum_{r=1}^n[(-1)^{r+1}-(-1)^r]=1-(-1)^n$ alternates between $0$ and $2$.
